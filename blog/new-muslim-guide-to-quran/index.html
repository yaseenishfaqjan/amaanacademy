A New Muslim's Guide to Learning the Quran from Zero

Start with the Arabic letters through Noorani Qaida, then learn the short surahs you need for prayer, beginning with Surah Al-Fatihah. Transliteration can help at first, but aim to read the Arabic itself. Go gently, at your own pace, with a patient one-on-one teacher.

If you have recently embraced Islam, first of all: congratulations, and welcome. Choosing to learn the Quran is one of the most beautiful steps on your new journey, and it is completely normal to feel both excited and a little overwhelmed about where to begin. This guide is written for the complete beginner who cannot yet read a single Arabic letter. There is no assumed knowledge here, no rush, and no judgement — only a clear, gentle path from zero.

Where should a new Muslim start?

The honest answer is: start at the very beginning, with the Arabic letters themselves. Almost every Muslim who reads the Quran fluently began with a small primer called the Noorani Qaida, which teaches the letters, their sounds, and how they join into words. It is the alphabet and the training wheels in one, and it is designed precisely for someone starting from nothing.

A simple, realistic order looks like this: learn the letters and their sounds, then how they join and how the short vowels work, then begin reading short words and phrases, and finally read from the Quran itself. You can begin this foundation through structured Nazra and Qaida lessons online, which take you step by step without ever skipping ahead. Do not worry about how far away fluent reading feels today — every reader you admire once sat exactly where you are now.

Which surahs should I learn first?

Alongside learning to read, many new Muslims choose to memorise a few short surahs straight away, because these are the ones you need for your five daily prayers. Being able to pray is often the most pressing and most comforting goal in the early days, so it makes sense to prioritise it.

  • Surah Al-Fatihah — the opening chapter, recited in every single unit of prayer.
  • A few short surahs from the end of the Quran, such as Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas.
  • The short phrases of praise (tasbih) said while bowing and prostrating.

A patient teacher will help you learn these by listening, repeating and correcting, a little at a time. There is no need to memorise a large amount quickly; even one short surah learned correctly is a real and rewarded achievement.

Can I learn with English and transliteration at first?

Yes. In the very beginning, transliteration — Arabic sounds written in English letters — can be a helpful bridge, especially so you can start praying without waiting months. There is nothing wrong with leaning on it at the start, and your lessons themselves can be explained entirely in English so every instruction is clear.

That said, transliteration has real limits: it cannot capture several Arabic sounds accurately, and relying on it long-term can lock in mispronunciation. So treat it as a temporary support, not the destination. As your reading grows through the Qaida, you will gently move from the transliteration to the Arabic script itself, which is always the goal. Learning the actual letters is what lets you one day open any page of the Quran and read it correctly.

Should I learn the basics of prayer alongside the Quran?

For most new Muslims, yes, and the two fit together naturally. The surahs you are learning are the very words you recite in prayer, so learning them while also learning the movements and sequence of salah means each reinforces the other. A good teacher can weave in the practical essentials — how to perform wudu, the order of the prayer, and what is said at each stage — so that learning to read is not separate from learning to worship.

This keeps your learning connected to daily life and to the reason you began. Rather than a dry academic exercise, it becomes a living part of your new faith from the first week.

Will I be judged for being a complete beginner?

This worry stops many new Muslims from starting, and it is completely understandable — but please be reassured. In a private, one-on-one lesson there is no class to sit in front of, no one to compare yourself to, and no audience for your mistakes. It is just you and a patient teacher who fully expects a beginner and meets you exactly where you are.

The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) taught that the one who reads the Quran with difficulty, stumbling over it, has a double reward. Your effort as a beginner is honoured, not judged. A caring teacher repeats a sound as many times as you need, encourages every small win, and never makes you feel rushed or embarrassed. If you would feel more comfortable, our female Quran teacher is available for sisters.

How do I choose the right teacher?

The teacher matters more than almost anything else, because patience and clarity are what carry a beginner through the early weeks. Look for a few simple things when you choose:

  • A qualified, named teacher — ideally a certified Aalim or Hafiz — not an anonymous tutor.
  • Genuine one-on-one lessons, so the full attention is on you.
  • The ability to explain clearly in a language you understand, such as English.
  • Warmth and patience with complete beginners, and no pressure.
  • A free trial, so you can feel whether the teaching suits you before committing.

A trial lesson tells you more than any description. When you experience a teacher explaining a letter gently and encouraging you to try again, you will quickly know whether it is the right fit.

How long will it take to read the Quran?

Every learner is different, so treat any timeline as a gentle guide rather than a target. With consistent one-on-one lessons a few times a week, many adult beginners work through the Qaida foundation over a few months and begin reading from the Quran after that. Learning the short surahs you need for prayer can happen much sooner, often within the first weeks, because you are memorising a small amount by ear.

What matters most is steady, regular practice rather than speed. Ten focused minutes most days will carry you further than a long session once in a while. Be patient and kind with yourself: you are learning the language of your faith, and every lesson is progress.

How do I stay motivated?

Motivation naturally rises and falls, and quieter weeks are normal for everyone. A few simple habits help keep you going:

  • Remember your why — you are learning to read the words of your Creator, and every letter is rewarded.
  • Set small, reachable goals, such as one new surah or one new group of letters.
  • Keep a fixed lesson time so it becomes a settled part of your week.
  • Lean on your teacher during slow stretches; a word of encouragement restores momentum.
  • Make dua for ease, and be gentle with yourself after a missed day — simply return the next.

Progress in the Quran is measured by steady, sincere return, not by never pausing. Keep coming back, and it will grow.

Common worries of new Muslims, and gentle reassurance

Many reverts carry the same quiet fears, and almost all of them melt away once lessons begin. “I'm too old to start” — you are not; adults learn beautifully, often with more focus than children. “Everyone else knows so much more than me” — in a private lesson there is no one to compare with, and your teacher began as a learner too. “What if I pronounce something wrong?” — that is exactly what the teacher is there for, to correct gently and kindly. “I don't have much time” — even two or three short lessons a week build real, lasting progress.

You belong, you are welcome, and you have already taken the hardest step by embracing Islam. Learning the Quran is simply the next gentle one. Frequently Asked Questions

I just became Muslim. Where do I start with the Quran?

Start with the Arabic letters through the Noorani Qaida, and learn the short surahs you need for prayer (beginning with Surah Al-Fatihah) alongside. A patient one-on-one teacher builds a gentle, step-by-step plan around you, assuming no prior knowledge at all.

Which surahs should a new Muslim learn first?

Most new Muslims begin with Surah Al-Fatihah, since it is recited in every prayer, followed by a few short surahs such as Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas, plus the short phrases said in bowing and prostration. These let you pray as soon as possible.

Can I use English transliteration to learn at first?

Yes, as a temporary bridge so you can start praying quickly. But transliteration cannot capture all Arabic sounds accurately, so treat it as a short-term support and aim to read the Arabic script itself as your reading grows through the Qaida.

Will I be judged for being a complete beginner?

No. In a private one-on-one lesson there is no class and no comparison, only a patient teacher who expects a beginner and corrects gently. The one who reads the Quran with difficulty is doubly rewarded, so your effort is honoured, not judged.

How long does it take a new Muslim to read the Quran?

With consistent one-on-one lessons a few times a week, many adult beginners complete the Qaida foundation over a few months and then begin reading from the Quran. The short surahs for prayer can be learned within the first weeks. Steady practice matters more than speed.

Can I have a female teacher as a new Muslim sister?
